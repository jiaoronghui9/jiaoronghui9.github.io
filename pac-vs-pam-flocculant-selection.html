PAC vs PAM: How to Choose the Right Flocculant

One of the most common questions we hear from water treatment plants in Vietnam and Indonesia: do I need PAC, PAM, or both? The short answer is that they are not competitors — they do two different jobs, and most plants get the best result using them together. This guide explains what each one does, when to use which, and what it costs.

What PAC does (the coagulant)

PAC (polyaluminum chloride) is an inorganic coagulant. Suspended particles in water — clay, algae, organic matter — carry negative surface charges and repel each other, which is why they stay suspended. PAC releases aluminum species that neutralize these charges, so fine particles destabilize and clump into small, visible flocs called "microflocs." PAC works fast (within minutes), is inexpensive, and performs well across a wide pH and temperature range, which makes it the default first step in most clarification trains.

What PAM does (the flocculant)

PAM (polyacrylamide) is an organic flocculant — a long-chain synthetic polymer. Its molecules are millions of units long; they adsorb onto several particles at once and physically bridge them into large, dense, fast-settling flocs. PAM does not create flocs from clear water — it needs the microflocs already formed by a coagulant (or naturally present solids, as in mining tailings) to grab onto. Used alone, it is the workhorse for sludge dewatering, where cationic PAM conditions the sludge before a belt press or centrifuge.

When you need PAC only, PAM only, or both

PAC only — simple clarification

If your goal is turbidity removal in a sedimentation tank or DAF unit and the flocs settle acceptably, PAC alone is usually enough. Powder PAC (Al2O3 28-31%, US $230–350 /ton FOB) is the general-purpose choice; liquid PAC (10-12% Al2O3, US $150–190 /ton) suits plants with on-site storage that want to skip dissolving steps.

PAM only — sludge and high-solids streams

Sludge dewatering, thickened mining tailings and coal-washing circuits already carry high solids — a cationic (for organic sludge) or anionic (for mineral slurry) PAM alone is the right tool. Adding PAC here usually just increases chemical cost and sludge volume.

Both together — the standard train for difficult water

High-turbidity surface water, dyeing effluent, food-processing wastewater: dose PAC first to destabilize, then a low dose of anionic PAM 30-60 seconds later to grow the microflocs into settleable ones. Plants typically report PAC savings of 20-40% when a small PAM dose is added, because the coagulant no longer has to do the flocculation job alone. The exact doses come from a jar test — never from a formula alone.
